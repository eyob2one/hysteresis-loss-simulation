Theoretical Foundations & Definitions

A comprehensive reference guide to magnetic core behavior, parameters, and loss separation models built from the ground up.

1. Ground-Up Microscopic Foundations

Magnetic Dipoles

Microscopic atomic-level magnetic fields created by electron spins. In ferromagnetic materials, quantum forces cause adjacent dipoles to snap into parallel alignment.

Magnetic Domains

Microscopic clustered regions where all internal dipoles point the same way. In an unmagnetized core, these domains point in random directions, canceling each other out.

Bloch Walls

The narrow transition boundary zones that separate adjacent magnetic domains pointing in different directions.

2. Core Magnetization Definitions

Magnetic Flux Density (B)

Measured in Teslas (T). It quantifies the strength and concentration of the magnetic field induced inside the material. One Tesla is equal to one Weber per square meter.

Magnetizing Field Strength (H)

Measured in Ampere-turns per meter (A/m). It is the external magnetizing force applied to the material, usually driven by current passing through a wire winding around the core:

H = (N * I) / l
Saturation Flux Density (Bsat)

The maximum limit of flux density that a core can hold. Beyond Bsat, increasing the external field H yields no significant increase in magnetization, as all internal magnetic domains are already fully aligned.

Remanence (Br)

The remaining magnetic flux density left in the core when the external magnetizing field H is brought back to zero. It represents the "magnetic memory" or permanent magnetization of the material.

Coercivity (Hc)

The reverse magnetic field strength required to completely demagnetize the material, bringing its flux density B back to zero. A material with low Hc is "soft" (easy to magnetize/demagnetize), while high Hc indicates a "hard" permanent magnet.

Peak Applied Field (Hmax)

The maximum amplitude of the magnetizing field strength applied during the alternating current (AC) cycle.

3. Hysteresis Loop & Loss Mechanisms

Because moving domain walls pin irreversibly against physical material defects, the magnetization path demagnetizes along a separate lag path rather than a straight line.

Hysteresis Loop Area

Ferromagnetic materials do not magnetize and demagnetize along the same path. This lag creates a closed B-H loop. The area enclosed by the loop represents the energy dissipated as heat per unit volume per cycle (measured in Joules per cubic meter, J/m³).

Total Core Loss Power

The rate of energy loss in the core. For an active volume Vcore (in cm³) and frequency f, the power loss is computed as: Ploss = Loop Area × f × Vcore × 10⁻⁶

4. AC Loss Separation (Bertotti Model)

Core losses increase rapidly with frequency. Under the Bertotti model, the total power loss density is split into three physical components:

Hysteresis Loss (Ph)

Caused by microscopic friction during domain wall movements. It scales linearly with frequency: Ph = Wcycle × f

Eddy Current Loss (Pe)

Caused by circulating electrical currents induced inside conductive core materials by the alternating magnetic field. It scales quadratically with frequency: Pe = ke × f² × Bpeak²

Excess Loss (Pex)

Arises from the dynamic, non-uniform motion of magnetic domains under high frequencies. It scales by the 1.5 power of frequency: Pex = kex × f1.5 × Bpeak1.5

5. Empirical Steinmetz Fit

The Steinmetz equation is an empirical model used by electrical engineers to quickly estimate core loss density based on test data.

Ploss = k × fα × Bpeakβ

Where k is the Steinmetz coefficient, α is the frequency exponent, and β is the flux density exponent. The Steinmetz ML Fit tab runs a regression analysis over custom test data points to compute these exponents for any core material.
